## Method, source convention and central position

This document addresses the **10 July 2017 bedside protest as central firsthand evidence**: the patient states that he summoned the treating consultant while still an inpatient, objected that the presenting foot condition had not been adequately investigated, received a reply concerning access to podiatry, and was discharged on **11 July**. The signed statement was made on **2 September 2026**. Its statement date is not the date of the encounter. The completed specialist account **does not record that objection**. The account is neither relegated to an appendix nor converted into a hypothetical; the question-set asks the expert to examine its medical and documentary significance directly. This compilation does not adjudicate witness credibility, motive or intention.

**The essential distinction:** (1) a material *objection raised during ongoing inpatient care*, (2) the *response clinically required while the consultant could still act*, (3) the *decision to discharge the following day*, (4) the *omission of the objection from the completed consultant account*, and (5) the *subsequent years of care by the author of that account* are separate but connected subjects for expert analysis. The documentary omission may be clinically significant even if an expert ultimately concludes that the management itself was within acceptable practice. The clinical criticism may be supportable on other 2017 findings independently of the protest.

**Documentary representation is an affirmative question, not just an omission question.** The final account does more than remain silent about the 10 July objection: it supplies a positive account of what the patient understood, what the clinician inferred from his description of foot pain and leg tension, how the extended admission and counselling served treatment planning, and what follow-up was proposed. The expert must compare what the patient communicated on 3 and 10 July with what this completed GP-facing account positively conveyed. Its combination of omission **and** affirmative representation could affect future diagnostic framing and responsibility for reassessment without implying deliberate deception or a predetermined medical deficiency.

**Working sources:** The repository's 40 numbered source transcriptions, its orientation and all 56 matters, the three 3 July interview-page transcriptions and signed statement transcription are accepted as the textual basis, as requested by the patient. Primary scans are not independently authenticated here; this is not a credibility caveat about the bedside account. The composite July hospital narrative includes material recorded or finalised after 11 July, so decision-point chronology must be preserved. The available compilation does not itself contain all daily ward chart entries, audit histories or a complete PAR. No contents are invented for absent records.

## Part I — The clinical problem presented in June 2017

The 29 June 2017 GP referral (M2) documents a linked presentation: bilateral corns/calluses and other painful foot concerns, an unusual stiff/festinant gait, muscular stiffness and increased upper- and lower-limb tone, trouble initiating movement, difficulty relaxing in bed accompanied by **toe clawing**, functional and occupational decline, worsening dexterity and suspected Parkinsonism or another neurological disorder. The GP's observation that the patient had initially attributed gait problems to foot lesions is not a specialist conclusion that those lesions independently explained the gait.

The receiving neurologist's 3 July interview (M5–M7) records a history of longstanding posturing/tension and distinct sensations of foot pain, earlier calluses and fallen arches, and relief of some pain after less occupational standing in approximately 2012. **Different sensations do not demonstrate independent causes**: central involuntary posturing, rigidity, altered gait mechanics, fixed hammertoes/pes planus, corns, peripheral nerve disease and mixed nociceptive/neuropathic pain can coexist. Equally, Parkinsonism does not by itself establish a dystonic cause for every painful foot lesion.

The hospital's documented examination (M4) identifies substantial bradykinesia, lead-pipe rigidity, little resting tremor, absent arm swing, striking gait, a footdrop-like appearance **suggestive of dystonic posture rather than true footdrop**, pes planus and hammertoes. These findings justified the extensive young-onset Parkinsonism workup and consideration of PRKN/PARK2. But the recorded coexistence of structural abnormalities and a potentially dystonic gait specifically raises the question whether toe posture and abnormal loading were explored in relation to pressure pain. The hypothesis of separate foot pathology or peripheral neuropathy was explicitly uncertain (`Not clear`), and the July narrative does not itself set out compelling positive neuropathy findings. It would be equally wrong to declare neuropathy absent without examination and investigations.

The central expert problem is therefore **not** whether the Parkinsonism diagnosis was achieved; it is whether the original painful-foot, toe-clawing and lower-limb posturing presentation was sufficiently **characterised, reasoned about, managed or assigned for specific follow-up** by the time the patient raised his direct concern and was discharged. Recognition of symptoms, diagnosis of Parkinsonism, referral to podiatry, and explanation of the *relationship* between symptoms are different clinical acts.

### 10–13 July: distinguish clinical knowledge from document provenance

1. **Before the 10 July objection:** M2 and M5–M7 are independently dated to June and 3 July; M4 describes examination findings, neuroimaging, a complex-case discussion and treatment thinking, but the assembled narrative does not prove the drafting date of each sentence. The separate notation of results updated **9 July at 16:12** is an identifiable stage, not proof that every clinical paragraph was settled then.
2. **10 July, still inpatient:** S26 places the patient's express disagreement about the uninvestigated presenting feet directly before Monaghan. This is clinically material notice given before discharge, in addition to the clinician's earlier findings, not a post-discharge complaint. The 10 July MRI and abdominal imaging do not themselves resolve the painful-toe phenotype.
3. **11 July, discharge:** The hospital record reports discharge with Parkinsonism management, rehabilitation and a requested/attempted podiatry route. The available compiled summary does not identify a documented direct response to the bedside objection, a focused neurological disposition of painful clawing, or the specific clinician responsible for resolving it. That is a finding about the *available record*, not an assertion that a nonexistent record has been proved.
4. **13 July, post-discharge documentation:** The DaTscan was completed and the addendum expressly records transcription finalisation at **21:41** (M8/M4). Earlier consultation notes and a **9 July 16:12** results update appear in the assembled narrative. Whether the key foot-interpretation, patient-attribution and plan sentences were first drafted before or after the bedside objection—and who approved any revision—cannot be reconstructed from this flattened transcription. **Transcription finalisation is not synonymous with proof of a separate consultant sign-off**, and no later confirmation time should be invented.
5. **The completed clinical representation:** The final product omits the bedside objection while affirmatively presenting the patient's supposed clear separation of sensations, the clinician's possible neuropathy/mechanical interpretation, the benefits of extended counselling, his understanding of levodopa deferral, and a staged plan with podiatry/neurology follow-up. The expert should evaluate the cumulative meaning imparted to a reader, not just count whether the event was mentioned.

**Bedside sequence — what the expert must answer directly.** The omission of the objection is a **separate observable discrepancy** between S26 and the finished M4 clinical account; read together with the account’s affirmative presentation of patient understanding and clinical disposition it also poses a question of **potentially materially incomplete or misleading representation to later readers**. The medical expert should determine whether this was *material clinical information* for handover and continuity, and what contemporaneous response a responsible consultant should have made. They should not be asked to decide whether the omission was intentional. The contemporaneous problem existed before the protest; the protest brought it directly to the consultant's attention while clinical action remained possible.

**The 3 July semantic comparison.** M7 records that foot pain and leg tension did not feel the same, in the setting of reduced occupational loading. M4 subsequently says the patient had `markedly divorced` the phenomena and advances possible secondary neuropathy (perhaps associated with past alcohol exposure). The expert must distinguish (a) accurate reporting of different *sensations*, (b) clinician's permissible *hypothesis of different mechanisms*, and (c) any unsupported attribution to the patient of a settled *aetiological separation*. The later use of the term `burning` should be assessed for its original provenance where possible; its appearance in a compiled note alone cannot establish when or by whom it was first expressed.

## Part IV — Earliest potential clinical failure: recognition, objection, response, discharge and record

### Four distinct early thresholds

**Recognition — 29 June / 3 July.** The GP supplied a linked painful-foot/toe-clawing and movement presentation; the neurologist personally recorded a gait suggestive of dystonic posturing alongside hammertoes and pes planus. There was enough to **recognise a specific differential question**, at least by the specialist assessment on **3 July**. That is a high-confidence finding about clinical relevance, not a finding that assessment had already failed on that date.

**Action — during 3–10 July.** Depending on pain severity, functional effects, examination and competing priorities, appropriate action might include examining flexible versus fixed clawing, toe/ankle position at rest and while walking, pain and pressure distribution, muscle contraction with posture, sensory loss and ankle reflexes, circulation, footwear/weight-bearing, and dynamic variation. Some examination could occur promptly; specialty assessment, orthoses, peripheral neuropathy studies or an observed movement examination might be deferred with accountable follow-up. Whether all were necessary is a historical clinical judgment, not a checklist automatically constituting an obligation.

**Direct objection and response — 10 July.** S26 places the clinical concern **personally before the treating consultant while he remained responsible for the inpatient episode**. The question becomes more precise than an abstract failure of diagnostic completeness: What did the condition and the direct objection require the neurologist to do *before the next day's discharge*? A proportionate response could have meant clarification of remaining symptoms; examining or reviewing posture, loading and sensory signs; explaining known and unknown causes; obtaining advice; identifying who would investigate what; modifying or confirming discharge with explicit reasons and follow-up; and documenting the objection and response. It did **not** require an instant confirmed dystonia diagnosis or a mandatory levodopa trial. Conversely, a generic statement about access to podiatry would need assessment against the broader neurological question and the adequacy of the actual arrangements.

**Discharge — 11 July.** This is the strongest opportunity to examine whether any clinically material concern remained without sufficient explanation, assessment or safe pathway. The podiatry attempt, rehabilitation, ongoing neurological care, counselling and legitimate treatment planning support the clinicians' position. What these do not, without more, establish is *who owned the question of painful toe-clawing and abnormal dystonic-looking posture* and what would trigger reassessment. The earliest defensible conclusion of care outside a reasonable range may fall on **10 July** if an expert finds the response itself clinically insufficient at that active decision point; it may fall at **11 July** if unacceptable discharge disposition is first demonstrable then; or it may be unsupported if staged care was adequate. There is no need to presume 11 July is automatically earliest.

**Final documentation — 13 July.** M4 records a substantial clinical history and treatment rationale, describes the patient as having `markedly divorced` foot pain from tension, uses a provisional neuropathy formulation, and does not record the direct objection. The **omission itself is a discrete documentary issue**. The questions are: was the objection material to the clinical picture, was the completed account sufficiently candid about an unresolved question and the patient's actual expressed position, and could the record influence subsequent clinical interpretation? This can be clinically material **even if** the consultant's plan was reasonable. A movement-disorders neurologist can assess likely clinical significance; authorship history, evidence handling and intention require other disciplines.

### What the final account *positively* says, and why that matters

The clinical expert should audit five affirmative messages in the completed M4 rather than treating its omission as a purely clerical gap: **(1)** a first-person symptom-history synthesis in which the patient supposedly distinguished leg tension from foot pain (`in his own mind`, `markedly divorced`); **(2)** the clinician’s provisional view that the corns, calluses and “burning” might be separate or neuropathic, possibly alcohol-related (`may`, `perhaps`, `Not clear`); **(3)** the explanation that the extended admission allowed questions to be explored and a firmer treatment plan developed; **(4)** the claim that the patient understood the rationale for deferring levodopa and was counselled over multiple issues (which should not be inflated into agreement with the foot assessment); and **(5)** podiatry referral, rehabilitation, gradual medication introduction, Parkinson’s nurse contact and neurology follow-up presented as a continuing plan, ultimately `As per above`.

The expert's question is whether a future GP or neurologist reading that package **without the direct 10 July objection** would reasonably infer a more settled attribution of foot symptoms, more satisfactory resolution of the patient's concern or more complete assignment of neurological reassessment than the underlying clinical history supports. The objection's clinical materiality and any skew to continuing care are examinable independently of motive and independently of whether the original treatment choices were otherwise reasonable. Conversely, explicit provisional wording and actual podiatry arrangements may reduce the risk of misleading clinical inference. Neither conclusion should be presumed.

The **creation sequence** cannot be flattened: the 3 July interview and 9 July results notation predate the protest; the 10 July objection predates discharge; discharge occurred 11 July; the DaTscan and marked transcription finalisation occurred 13 July. The evidence does **not** date the first drafting of each sentence or establish a separate consultant confirmation timestamp. The critical provenance question is what the author knew when the materially relevant positive statements were made or approved, not merely the date printed on the final addendum.

### Test the strongest adverse proposition against reasonable alternatives

**Proposition to be tested, without euphemism:** On 10 July 2017, after direct notice that the original foot presentation remained inadequately investigated, the treating neurologist did not adequately respond to a clinically material problem while the patient was still in hospital; discharge proceeded on 11 July and the completed specialist account omitted that direct objection. An independent expert must determine **whether**, and **from which exact date**, the medical response or subsequent disposition fell outside the range of reasonable 2017 neurological practice.

**Reasons that could support concern:** (i) the GP's linked complaint; (ii) dystonic-looking gait recorded by the neurologist; (iii) painful lesions and structural deformity potentially related to abnormal loading; (iv) uncertain neuropathy without a clearly recorded sensory basis; (v) tension treated nocturnally with clonazepam but toe-clawing/pain connection not evidently resolved; (vi) direct protest *before* discharge; (vii) lack of a clearly described responsible neurological reassessment pathway; and (viii) objection omitted from the final handover. The combination is more informative than any individual quotation.

**Reasons that could defeat or limit it:** (i) adequate focused examination may have taken place elsewhere in the chart; (ii) structural corns/hammertoes and occupational pressure could explain substantial pain; (iii) separate neuropathy was plausible rather than excluded; (iv) podiatry/rehabilitation and GP referrals are genuine responses; (v) clinician could reasonably defer advanced phenotyping or investigations while arranging timely review; (vi) Parkinsonism diagnosis, scans and genetic differential were substantial legitimate work; (vii) immediate levodopa had known trade-offs in young onset; (viii) the omission from a summary does not alone establish no bedside explanation or all clinically relevant information was lost from the continuing record. **None of these is a substitute for locating the actual disposition of the feet.**
