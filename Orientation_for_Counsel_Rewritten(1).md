# The Unanswered Referral and the Authority of the Record

*Orientation for independent counsel — Galway neurological care, 2017–2026*

**The case to be examined is not whether Parkinsonism was diagnosed, whether the hospital performed substantial investigations, or whether the original consultant can explain his choice of medication. It is whether the hospital ever adequately answered the connected clinical problem referred by the GP on 29 June 2017—and what occurred when the patient, still in hospital, directly challenged the completeness of that assessment before discharge.**

There is a second, inseparable question. The treating neurologist was not simply a later reader of the discharge summary. He was the clinician who interviewed the patient, observed abnormalities suggestive of dystonic foot posturing, formulated the competing explanation of possible neuropathy, and, according to the patient's signed account, received the pre-discharge protest. The completed record did not disclose that reported protest. It used disputed symptom wording in explaining the feet, and it described the prolonged admission in favourable terms. The same neurologist continued to participate in the patient's care and, in 2025, defended the historical management. Galway University Hospitals subsequently maintained a no-deficit position through its Quality and Patient Safety process.

Counsel is therefore being asked to examine a potential **failure of clinical care compounded by a failure of clinical representation and subsequent institutional validation**. The most serious allegation is knowing material misrepresentation during an active duty of care, followed by continuing reliance upon the resulting account. That is an allegation for investigation, not a finding already adjudicated. The directly visible documentary question is whether the hospital's extensive record can identify a reasoned, integrated disposition of the original painful-foot and movement presentation. Its volume cannot supply an answer missing from its content.

## The vantage point: put the first and last institutional documents side by side

Begin with **29 June 2017**, the GP's urgent referral. Then read **26 January 2026**, the Quality and Patient Safety letter maintaining the institutional position that the earlier findings of no deficit remained appropriate. Everything between those dates must answer a simple question: **Where did the hospital assess, explain, manage, or explicitly plan to resolve the painful feet and toe clawing as part of the connected neurological presentation it received?**

The original GP referral described a patient whose journey had begun with painful feet in April. The GP followed the development of the presentation over months and documented toe clawing, gait disturbance, stiffness, abnormal tone and movement-initiation difficulties. That was not a retrospectively reconstructed symptom cluster. It was the referring clinician's contemporaneous clinical account. It remains independent of the hospital's subsequent interpretation.

The January 2026 correspondence, by contrast, defended care with prominent reference to neurological treatment reasoning, including caution in initiating Sinemet. The clinical record certainly demonstrates investigation and treatment of Parkinsonism, but that is not identical to a demonstrated disposition of the painful feet, clawing and abnormal posture. Nor is the assertion that alternative diagnoses were considered equivalent to showing how the particular original problem was investigated. The appropriate institutional question is whether governance reviewed the full referral-to-disposition trajectory, rather than chiefly the defensibility of the established Parkinsonism management plan.

**A hospital may successfully identify one major disease while failing to address a significant manifestation or connected presenting complaint. The success of the diagnosis does not authenticate the completeness of the admission.** Likewise, an institutional conclusion of no deficit is a conclusion requiring reasons; it cannot retroactively perform an unidentifiable assessment.

## One patient, one GP, one developing clinical problem

On **10 April 2017**, the patient attended primary care with bilateral foot pain and an abnormal gait. The feet were the entry point into medical care, not an incidental issue introduced years later. Corns, calluses, painful pressure areas and toe abnormalities appeared within a developing history. Over the next months the GP observed worsening movement, increased tone and stiffness, impaired dexterity and functional deterioration. The patient described toe clawing and difficulty relaxing his muscles. The urgent referral on 29 June brought the foot symptoms and the neurological abnormalities together.

The referral did not ask the hospital simply to confirm Parkinsonism. It presented a clinically connected problem whose cause and proper disposition required specialist judgment. It did not guarantee that a single cause would explain every symptom. It did make the relationship between those symptoms a matter needing examination, reasoned separation, or an explicit plan for further assessment.

This is the controlling comparison throughout. A later record can supply a legitimate answer by establishing a separate foot condition, showing a neurological connection, or explaining why uncertainty remained and who would follow it up. What it cannot do is transform the reasonableness of another clinical decision into proof that the referral's entire question was addressed.

## The first transformation: the hospital précis

At the hospital's entry point, the GP's integrated account was compressed into a précis foregrounding movement initiation, increased tone, stiffness and cogwheeling. The original painful-foot emphasis and toe clawing were not preserved there. The abbreviated statements were not necessarily inaccurate in what they said; the question is what they ceased to communicate.

The distinction is causally important. A case introduced as rigidity, bradykinesia and suspected Parkinsonism invites an account of movement-disorder diagnosis and dopaminergic management. A case introduced as **painful feet, toe clawing and abnormal gait within an emerging movement disorder** invites that work *and* a focused explanation of the feet. Those are different frames for the same patient.

The existence of the full GP referral in the records does not establish that every decision-maker read it. Equally, the abbreviated précis does not prove nobody did. The evidential task is to reconstruct who received the full referral, what the initial team and the case meeting were actually shown, and whether the original question governed decisions. The source-to-summary discrepancy itself needs no speculation: the documents can be compared directly.

## The 4 July decision: senior expertise acting upon what information?

During the early admission—identified in the patient's chronology as **4 July 2017**—the case was discussed within the Neurology Department's complex-case process. Dr Thomas Monaghan's subsequent account identifies advice from Professor Timothy Counihan about caution in commencing or escalating levodopa in a younger person with Parkinson's disease. Monaghan's 2017 correspondence to Professor Daniel Healy also refers to his and “Tim's” wish to hold back levodopa if possible.

This was consequential specialist input. Counihan was a senior academic neurologist in the Galway environment, with published interests bearing directly upon young-onset Parkinsonism, PRKN disease, dystonia and the assessment of painful motor phenomena. Research involving Galway clinicians before and around 2017 makes clear that these were not foreign concepts to the department. His advice could carry substantial practical weight without being formal hospital policy.

The issue is **the informational foundation and scope of that advice**. Advice on when to begin levodopa can be clinically sound and still fail to answer an unpresented or separately unresolved question about painful foot clawing. The 2025 account establishes senior involvement in a treatment decision; it does not by itself establish that Counihan reviewed the entire GP letter, personally assessed the feet, or was asked to resolve their relationship to dystonia. Nor does his May 2025 statement that he was not involved in the patient's care erase the acknowledged departmental advice: participation in a case discussion and formal responsibility for ongoing care are different roles.

If the original referral was not brought before the senior discussion, an influential treatment direction may have been formed from a narrowed representation. If it was presented, counsel should ask for the reasoning by which the painful-foot component was addressed or set aside. **Seniority gives weight to specialist judgment; it does not add facts that may not have been supplied to the judgment-maker.**

## Eight days: what did the admission accomplish for the feet?

The patient entered hospital on **3 July 2017** and was discharged on **11 July**. Extensive neurological investigation followed. Parkinsonism was identified, genetic possibilities including Parkin/PRKN were considered, and medication strategy received careful attention. These clinical activities are not disputed and should be documented in counsel's account.

The hospital also recorded relevant lower-limb information. The neurologist's examination described a foot-drop appearance **somewhat suggestive of dystonic posture, explicitly not true foot drop**. The history contained corns, calluses, fallen arches and painful feet; podiatry involvement was sought. These facts prevent the simplistic assertion that nobody ever mentioned or attended to the feet.

But **mentioning feet, providing podiatry care and investigating the neurological relationship between painful feet and abnormal posturing are not interchangeable**. Where is the focused recorded assessment of pain distribution, posture, toe clawing, functional impairment, possible dystonic mechanism and competing sensory or mechanical explanations? Where is the documented reconciliation of the referral's toe clawing with the examination's dystonic-looking posture? Where is the reasoned disposition—diagnosis, explicit uncertainty with follow-up, or a justified separation of the problem?

The record contains a tentative suggestion that some symptoms might be separate or represent peripheral neuropathy. A differential diagnosis is a legitimate clinical act. But the tentative hypothesis must not be mistaken for the completed investigation of the question that prompted referral. A podiatry pathway could address lesions and pressure points while leaving the neurological cause of recurrent abnormal positioning unresolved.

The consultant described the longer admission as producing a **“firmer footing going forward.”** That formulation may well have applied to the Parkinsonism diagnosis and treatment plan. It does not demonstrate that the patient's original painful-foot problem had become materially clearer. **Duration is not a clinical outcome. A long admission is not evidence of a complete assessment unless its contents show what was actually resolved or appropriately handed forward.**

## The handwritten account and the origin of the neuropathy narrative

The neurologist's handwritten interview and subsequent typed synthesis are unusually revealing because they permit comparison between stages of professional representation. The patient described different experiences: pain associated with the feet, altered gait, bodily and leg tension, and the effect of work and prolonged standing. Distinguishing the *experience* of foot pain from the *experience* of leg tension does not itself establish that the conditions had separate causes.

The final synthesis went further. It characterised the patient as having “markedly divorced” leg tension from corns, calluses and foot pain. It entertained that the symptoms might be wholly separate or perhaps due to secondary peripheral neuropathy and added that the position was “Not clear.” In that same passage, it grouped **“corns and callouses and burning”**. The patient expressly denies having supplied *burning* as his description during the 2017 inpatient interview. He points instead to the original toe clawing and its importance to the movement presentation.

This is not a trivial vocabulary disagreement. Burning can serve as apparent clinical support for a neuropathic classification; clawing directs attention to abnormal posture and contraction. Both can occur in complex neurological disease, but they are not equivalent observations. The allegation is that the final account attributed patient-history support to an explanatory category without an adequate source in the original exchange. The independent comparison must identify exactly where each descriptor entered the record, whether from the patient, GP, another clinician, or the interpreting neurologist.

**An explanation cannot create its own evidential foundation by placing an inferred feature into the patient's reported history.** If the descriptor was supplied from another legitimate source, identify it. If it was clinician interpretation, distinguish it from the patient's words. If knowingly misattributed, the issue becomes one of professional record integrity, not merely an arguable diagnostic judgment.

## The 10 July protest: the active decision point

According to the patient's signed supplementary statement of **2 September 2026**, on **10 July 2017**—the day before discharge—he asked four medical students to summon the treating neurologist and challenged why his painful feet had not been adequately investigated. He recalls the “full NCT” and “bald tyres” analogy and a response referring to how readily the patient could obtain a podiatry appointment.

The signed account is retrospective and should be identified as such. But its allegation is concrete: a specified bedside encounter, a specified clinician, identifiable potential witnesses, and an objection directed precisely at the original referral's unresolved foot problem. The statement was made in 2026; the encounter it describes was alleged to have occurred while the patient was still an inpatient in 2017. The chronology must not be conflated.

**The protest is not merely evidence that a patient was unhappy.** It alleges that the treating neurologist was directly informed that the clinical task initially presented by the GP had not been completed. By then the department had examined the patient, considered Parkinsonism, engaged senior advice and formulated a management approach. The patient was not asking whether the professor was correct about levodopa; he was questioning whether the original painful-foot problem had been investigated at all to a satisfactory degree.

What remained alive at that moment? The patient was physically present. The full GP referral could be retrieved. The neurological examination already contained dystonic-looking foot findings. The clinical team could assess the feet again, explain the competing mechanisms, reconsider the information put to senior colleagues, arrange appropriate further specialist review or document a sound plan for an unresolved question. Discharge might still have been reasonable. But the reason and follow-up for the remaining problem would need to be intelligible.

**This is the possible conversion point: an earlier loss or misinterpretation of information becomes an expressly challenged deficiency, and the treating clinician has an immediate opportunity to correct the clinical process or the account of it.** The completed narrative does not document the protest as described by the patient, and the examined documents do not clearly demonstrate a focused reconsideration in response.

## The record and the clinician are not identical sources of knowledge

If the 10 July encounter occurred as described, the treating neurologist possessed material information not communicated by the final discharge narrative: the patient had challenged the adequacy of the foot investigation before leaving hospital. This distinction arises **at the time of the alleged encounter**, not from speculation about what somebody remembered eight years later.

The clinician was simultaneously a participant in the events and an author of the professional account used to communicate them. The completed document might accurately describe neurological investigations, medication discussions and patient understanding of the levodopa strategy while remaining incomplete about the patient's disagreement over the feet. These propositions can coexist; that is precisely why the omission matters. Understanding one treatment rationale does not mean accepting that the whole referral was resolved.

The discharge occurred on **11 July**; a discharge-message record gives **17 July 2017 at 19:30** as its confirmation time. The longer synthesis also incorporates information dated after discharge. The confirmation date does not establish exactly when every sentence was drafted. It does identify why creation, amendment, authorship and authentication histories deserve investigation. The issue is what the approved account conveyed and what its author knew when the relevant passages were adopted.

A later physician consulting that summary is not in the same position as the original treating consultant. The later reader may know only the record. The original author was responsible for reconciling his firsthand clinical information with the document he created. **A contested record cannot be independently validated merely by its author's later repetition of its conclusions.**

## What was carried forward after discharge

The patient left hospital with the same painful-foot condition still troubling him. The documentary account carried a possible explanatory separation of foot pain from Parkinsonism. That separation was uncertain in the original formulation: the neuropathy possibility was tentative, and the clinician expressly said the matter was not clear.

Subsequent care involved neurological review, medication changes and repeated descriptions of foot symptoms. An April 2018 observation concerning deterioration after withdrawal of procyclidine is particularly relevant to reassessing movement-related manifestations. Recurrent podiatry treatment and continuing foot pain were not merely repetitions of the old story; they supplied new opportunities to examine its assumptions. The original treating neurologist remained involved, so this was not simply a new clinician taking an inherited summary at face value.

The test for each later appointment is not whether a doctor wrote something about pain. It is whether the original uncertainty was **tested, confirmed, revised or left in place without an identified evidential basis**. The question is also whether ongoing foot symptoms led to appropriate consideration of movement-disorders expertise and whether any stated limitation in subspecialist expertise prompted referral or a reasoned decision not to refer.

Continuity of appointments is real medical care. It is not, without more, proof that the particular unresolved manifestation received an adequate clinical response. **A patient can be actively treated for Parkinsonism while the problem that first brought him to the GP remains insufficiently understood or managed.**

## How a hypothesis can become the premise of later care

In July 2017, the physician described some leg and foot symptoms as *perhaps* separate or secondary peripheral neuropathy, expressly saying the matter was not clear. Later clinical and complaint material described management of **“neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)”**. The distinction deserves a provenance audit.

A clinician can reasonably prescribe a medicine for suspected neuropathic pain without proving neuropathy. But therapeutic use does not confirm the mechanism for which it was prescribed. If later defences use the treatment category as though it established the earlier interpretation, the reasoning risks circularity. The label may remain useful in symptom management while still failing to answer the neurological question raised by toe clawing and dystonic-looking posture.

This is not an allegation that every later use of the term *neuropathic* was knowingly false. It is a demand to identify the evidence, if any, by which a provisional 2017 possibility gained the degree of certainty or explanatory weight attributed to it later. A longitudinal record can become internally coherent because its entries repeat one another. **Repetition is not independent confirmation.**

## The 2023 and 2025 clinical consequences

The human cost cannot be confined to a debate about wording. A March 2023 urgent GP referral documented severe bilateral foot and lower-leg pain, describing a death wish associated with pain and the GP's concern. Nursing correspondence shortly thereafter described debilitating pain despite substantial pregabalin treatment and the patient's report of having no quality of life. These were documented expressions of grave distress.

A 2025 neurology letter again described severe morning foot pain, included the patient's words **“I won't live with it”**, and discussed limited relief, medication and the possibility of chronic pain specialist involvement. The assembled record has not identified a corresponding safeguarding intervention responding to those statements. That is a documentary observation requiring checking against any additional clinical, telephone or safeguarding records; it is not a finding that no action occurred anywhere.

These events need independent examination whether or not the original pain was dystonic. They also show why a potentially inaccurate original representation was not a merely archival concern. The patient remained in substantial pain while the historical explanatory framework retained influence over ongoing descriptions and treatment decisions.

## The genetic and specialist context: what the department already knew

The eventual clinical history included recognition of dystonia and a February **2026** specialist finding of pathogenic PRKN variants, with Parkinsonism and dystonia attributed to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations. Current GeneReviews material recognises that lower-limb dystonia can appear early in PRKN-related disease and may remain relatively isolated for years. These later developments do not prove that every painful-foot symptom in July 2017 was dystonic, or that a genetic diagnosis was compulsory during that admission.

They do, however, make the earlier abnormalities important: the GP's toe clawing, the hospital's dystonic-looking foot posture and the department's contemporaneous consideration of young-onset PRKN-related Parkinsonism. Galway-affiliated academic neurologists, including Professor Counihan, had published on the intersection of Parkinsonism, dystonia and genetic disease before and around the period in question. This was an environment with relevant specialist knowledge.

The precise unanswered institutional question is not whether any professor could retrospectively have diagnosed the patient. It is **whether the expertise available was presented with and applied to the full problem, and whether the hospital explained why a potentially dystonic painful-foot condition was assessed as it was**. The academic status of senior contributors makes the information-flow question sharper, not a substitute for proving what any named person knew about this patient.

## The 2025 defence: its author is also a participant

In 2025, the patient formally challenged the historical diagnostic framing and the prolonged painful-foot course. Monaghan responded to the complaint and in a submission to the Medical Council referred to Counihan's 2017 advice on levodopa. A defence of a cautious treatment strategy is relevant evidence, but it is not independent verification that the original referral was fully assessed or that the discharge account faithfully represented the patient's objection.

The treating neurologist had taken the earlier history, documented dystonic-looking features, authored the explanatory synthesis, continued to treat the patient and later described the historical course. **His professional account therefore needs to be examined both as clinical evidence and as the product of conduct under scrutiny.** He cannot resolve a dispute about the provenance of patient words merely by citing the final synthesis in which those words appear.

The central questions for the 2025 response are whether it returned to the full GP referral; whether it explained the origin and support of the neuropathy language; whether it addressed the later-recognised relevance of dystonic posturing; and whether it considered any account of the 10 July objection supplied to it at that date. The signed Article 16 supplementary statement was not created until September 2026 and must not be retrospectively attributed to a reviewer in 2025. What the institution actually received, and when, is itself part of the evidential chain.

## January 2026: governance and the question it defended

The Quality and Patient Safety correspondence of **26 January 2026** maintained the previous review outcome after the patient reported a dystonia diagnosis. It referred to review through the Preliminary Assessment Report and Serious Incident Management Team pathway, and communicated that no deficit in care had been identified. Its stated reassurance prominently concerned caution in starting Sinemet and the consideration of alternatives.

That tells counsel something important about the reasons communicated. It does not show an integrated account of how the original painful feet, toe clawing and dystonic-looking posturing were assessed and disposed of. The institution's position may be supported by underlying materials not fully reproduced in the letter; those materials must be obtained. Their possible existence, however, cannot be treated as though their contents have been proved.

**The governance question is whether the hospital defended the adequacy of an answer without demonstrating that the original question had been answered.** If the review's evidential foundation was the 2017 clinical narrative, but that narrative's completeness and symptom provenance were in dispute, the review needed to test those issues rather than merely assess whether the treatment decisions recorded within the narrative seemed reasonable.

The letter's no-deficit conclusion should not be exaggerated into a literal admission that governance believed there was no risk of any kind. The meaningful criticism is sharper: the breadth of the conclusion requires an evidential foundation responsive to the entire referral, and the communicated reasons do not themselves demonstrate that foundation.

## Fifteen consequences of the original information loss

These consequences should be examined as a connected clinical and institutional pathway, not discounted one by one as unrelated minor deficiencies.

1. **The GP's longitudinal trajectory was diminished at entry.** Painful feet and toe clawing had preceded the hospital's Parkinsonism formulation and should remain the starting clinical question.
2. **A narrowed précis could shape what senior colleagues were asked.** The information put to the 4 July departmental discussion must be reconstructed rather than presumed complete.
3. **Relevant senior expertise may have been applied to a narrower task.** Caution about levodopa does not itself dispose of painful dystonic-looking foot posture.
4. **The hospital recorded a finding calling for integration.** A dystonic-looking gait appearance was observed alongside toe clawing documented by the GP.
5. **Podiatry care may have addressed consequences rather than mechanism.** Treatment of pressure lesions cannot automatically establish the neurological cause of recurrent abnormal foot positioning.
6. **The eight-day duration may confer undeserved appearance of completeness.** Length and volume of investigation do not replace an identifiable answer to the original problem.
7. **The 10 July protest was made before the clinical process closed.** It is alleged to have alerted the treating author of the record to the unresolved referral while correction remained possible.
8. **An omitted objection could alter how later readers understand patient agreement.** Understanding a levodopa decision is not agreement that the feet had been adequately investigated.
9. **Disputed symptom wording could materially affect differential diagnosis.** The source of *burning* and the handling of *clawing* must be traced, not assumed.
10. **An uncertain diagnosis may gain authority through repetition.** Prescribing for suspected neuropathic pain does not establish the mechanism of pain.
11. **The record's author possessed a different information state from later readers.** Personal participation in a reported encounter cannot be equated with innocent reliance on a summary prepared by someone else.
12. **Continuing care created renewed duties of reassessment.** Subsequent motor and medication-related observations supplied opportunities to revisit the original foot formulation.
13. **A disclaimer of specialist expertise raises a referral question.** It does not automatically answer why specialist input was or was not obtained for unresolved movement-related symptoms.
14. **Severe subsequent distress raises independent care and safeguarding questions.** The 2023 and 2025 statements require investigation of actual clinical and institutional responses, not inference from silence alone.
15. **The governance review may have inherited the original question's narrowing.** Defending Parkinsonism treatment decisions is not necessarily auditing whether the referral's painful-foot problem was ever adequately disposed of.

## The gravest allegation and the strongest documentary case

The **gravest allegation** is that the treating neurologist, while responsible for an active inpatient admission, knowingly adopted a materially misleading clinical account: attributing symptom language that the patient did not provide, failing to record a material pre-discharge challenge of the unresolved presenting problem, and permitting a completed narrative to convey a degree of clinical completeness that he knew had been challenged. The further allegation is that he subsequently maintained or relied upon that account in continuing care and professional defence, and that institutional review failed to test its foundation. If knowing falsification or concealment were established, the professional significance would be considerably more serious than diagnostic error or imperfect clerical practice.

The **strongest directly examinable documentary case** is narrower but substantial. The full GP referral and the hospital précis differ materially in how they present the painful feet and toe clawing. The hospital recorded an appearance suggestive of dystonic foot posturing. The final synthesis entertained an uncertain separate neuropathic explanation, using a symptom descriptor the patient disputes having supplied. The patient's signed retrospective statement describes an objection made to the treating consultant before discharge; that objection is not recorded in the completed account. Continuing care and the institutional no-deficit response are documented. In the examined material, an integrated clinical disposition of the original painful-foot presentation has not been clearly identified.

These are two different conclusions, not competing narratives. Counsel can investigate the gravest allegation without pretending that deliberate intention is proved merely by the documentary pattern. Conversely, the absence of a finding on subjective intention cannot be used to make the documented referral-to-disposition gap disappear.

## The work counsel should require

The most useful first deliverable is a **referral-to-disposition table**, using the 29 June GP referral as the controlling text and the complete clinical and governance collection as the source. For every material foot and movement feature, identify the dated examination, clinical reasoning, conclusion, responsibility and follow-up. Mark where the documentary answer is definite, provisional, transferred to another service or not identifiable. An answer should not be inferred from the existence of a scan, a medication prescription or a general conclusion that care was appropriate.

Next, reconstruct the original **information state**: the full GP referral versus the hospital précis, the attendees and material considered at the complex-case meeting, the handwritten interview, any podiatry findings, the neurological examination and the provenance of the typed synthesis. Obtain drafting, amendment and sign-off history where available. Establish what reports and objections were received by each reviewer, with dates; compare the full PAR/SIMT analysis with the reasoning communicated in January 2026.

Then test the alleged **10 July encounter** through the signed patient statement, any contemporaneous notes, discharge arrangements, possible student or other witness accounts, and the final clinical document. If the encounter is established, examine the clinical and documentation response actually undertaken. The question is not whether the patient could compel a preferred diagnosis. It is what the service did when the patient identified an apparently unresolved part of the original referral before discharge.

Finally, trace the **longitudinal effect** of the original neuropathy and Parkinsonism formulations through 2018–2025, including when dystonia was considered, which specialist assessments were obtained, how pain was managed and how the original diagnosis or discharge narrative entered later professional and governance explanations. An independent specialist should assess the historical standard of care, potential breach and causal consequences; counsel should assess professional obligations, available causes of action and evidentiary sufficiency separately.

## The issue for counsel

The central issue is not whether Galway neurology performed useful medical work. It did. Nor is the issue whether PRKN disease should necessarily have been genetically confirmed in July 2017. The original referral asked a broader question, and the hospital's subsequent clinical authority must be tested against that question.

**A patient entered a specialist hospital with a painful-foot and neurological presentation already integrated by his GP. The hospital's entry summary narrowed that presentation; its own examination recovered a potentially dystonic abnormality; an influential senior-informed treatment direction developed; and the patient says he directly challenged the absence of an adequate foot investigation before discharge. The final account did not communicate that reported challenge and used disputed history wording in a tentative alternative explanation. Its author continued to participate in care, later defended the history, and institutional governance maintained a no-deficit conclusion without, in the communicated reasons examined, identifying a satisfactory integrated answer to the original referral.**

The first task for counsel is therefore direct: **require the institution to identify where it answered the GP's painful-foot and toe-clawing referral, and show what the treating neurologist did when that apparent failure was reportedly brought to his attention before discharge.** The next task is to determine whether the clinical record faithfully represented what was known, said, unresolved and decided. The gravest professional allegation—knowing misrepresentation and concealment—must be tested there, where the original care and the authority of the record meet.

## Appendix — Documentary issues and controlling questions (56 matters)

This register accompanies the orientation as a working checklist for independent counsel. It reproduces the supplied source issues, adds a separate issue concerning the accuracy of attributed patient knowledge in the 2021 DBS discussion, and renumbers subsequent entries for chronological coherence. The issues are **questions to investigate**, not determinations of fault.

### **I. The original referral and hospital assessment — Issues 1–9**

1. **Referral materially compressed** — Compare the complete GP referral with the abbreviated hospital précis, including the relationships between the omitted symptoms.
2. **Triage premise potentially changed** — Determine whether the abbreviated presentation affected urgency, specialist involvement or assessment scope.
3. **Accountability for producing précis unidentified** — Establish who wrote, approved and used the précis, and whether the full referral remained linked to it.
4. **Specialist meeting source material unclear** — Establish what Professor Counihan actually read or was told at the July 2017 complex case meeting.
5. **Levodopa decision conflated with diagnostic question** — Examine whether the decision to defer medication displaced investigation of the dystonic-looking feet.
6. **Dystonic findings not fully integrated** — Compare the examination and DATscan indication with the eventual explanation of the painful toes and feet.
7. **Neurological examination potentially incomplete for question referred** — Assess whether the examination adequately addressed painful toe-clawing and foot abnormalities.
8. **Peripheral explanation introduced without demonstrated foundation** — Identify the positive evidence supporting peripheral neuropathy rather than merely a possible explanation.
9. **Alcohol differential potentially overweighted** — Trace how alcohol history entered the neuropathy hypothesis and how that explanation was subsequently used.

### **II. Construction of the record and discharge — Issues 10–17**

10. **Handwritten-to-typed change in meaning** — Distinguish the patient's description of different sensations from a clinician's conclusion that they represented separate medical problems.
11. **Final impression intensifies patient attribution** — Examine the basis for the statements “in his own mind” and “markedly divorced.”
12. **Bedside protest not entered in completed synthesis** — Compare the completed discharge record with the patient's account of the 10 July 2017 protest.
13. **Potential failure to react to protest** — Identify any subsequent examination, explanation, escalation or documented decision responding to that protest.
14. **Discharge plan carries forward uncertainty** — Establish what “As per above” actually resolved, assigned or left outstanding concerning the feet.
15. **Specialist referral scope arguably mismatched** — Distinguish the genetic enquiry involving Professor Healy from a directed investigation of painful dystonic feet.
16. **Podiatry pathway may have substituted for neurological ownership** — Determine who retained responsibility for investigating the relationship between toe-clawing, abnormal posture and pain.
17. **Document-finalisation sequence uncertain** — Recover original creation and modification metadata, including relevant additions and the timing of confirmation.

### **III. Later observations and opportunities for reassessment — Issues 18–22**

18. **Early procyclidine response insufficiently integrated** — Examine whether early medication responses altered the clinical interpretation of the foot abnormalities.
19. **April 2018 withdrawal observation not used to test separation** — Examine the rapid return of the outside-left-foot corn after procyclidine withdrawal and any subsequent reassessment.
20. **Subsequent correspondence may have inherited untested interpretation** — Trace repetition and strengthening of the neuropathic explanation in later letters.
21. **Alcohol references following abstinence require temporal audit** — Compare alcohol-related explanations with the actual chronology, including cessation.
22. **2021 levodopa pain response not reconciled with attribution** — Identify pain relief associated with levodopa and whether it prompted reconsideration of the neuropathy hypothesis.

### **IV. Patient safety — Issue 23**

23\. **March 2023 psychological-safety response unclear** — Identify the risk assessment, safety planning, communication and follow-up following the urgent GP warning about severe pain and a death wish.

### **V. The 2025 complaint and institutional review — Issues 24–33**

24. **Complaint recharacterised as solely clinical judgment** — Examine whether the handling of the March complaint adequately captured its documentary and patient-safety concerns.
25. **Initial reviewer was author of contested clinical account** — Identify what independent validation complemented the treating neurologist's response.
26. **QPS April explanation may narrow complaint** — Compare the Quality and Patient Safety characterisation with the full complaint.
27. **Escalation to IMF implies changed pathway without fully visible criteria** — Obtain the decision record and terms of reference for the subsequent review process.
28. **Claim of independent review requires validation** — Identify the Preliminary Assessment Report author, reviewers, potential conflicts and evidence available to them.
29. **June instruction to stop sending evidence may have constrained review** — Determine whether evidence submitted later was accepted or excluded, and with what effect.
30. **PAR source schedule undisclosed** — Obtain the full Preliminary Assessment Report, attachments, source inventory and questions examined.
31. **SIMT answer appears treatment-centric** — Distinguish the defence of Sinemet timing from the challenge to the original diagnostic attribution.
32. **SIMT did not visibly address compression/protest/As per above** — Determine whether the underlying review actually examined those foundational discrepancies.
33. **Potential failure to independently compare handwritten and typed accounts** — Identify whether the original interview, typed version and final clinical impression were independently compared.

### **VI. Record access, rectification and governance — Issues 34–45**

34. **July 2025 access search into bedside students not transparent** — Determine which records, student rosters and possible witness-identification sources were searched.
35. **Podiatry administrative routing may fragment record access** — Identify the relevant controller and ensure the podiatry material can be accessed in conjunction with the neurological record.
36. **Article 16 request described as erasure** — Compare the patient's request for rectification and annotation with the later response characterising it as erasure.
